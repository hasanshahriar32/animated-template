Soil first. Everything else follows.

Four rules every loam garden is built on, whether it is a courtyard behind a terrace or three acres on the edge of the Downs.

  1. Feed the soil, not the plant.

    We mulch with compost made from our own green waste, never peat, and leave out chemical feeds. Living soil holds water, stores carbon and does the feeding for you.

    Compost

  2. Keep every drop that falls.

    Rain gardens, gravel instead of tarmac and a water butt on every downpipe. A loam garden sends almost nothing into the drain and needs little watering by its second summer.

    Rainwater

  3. Plant for the whole food web.

    Native hedges, borders that flower from March to November and seedheads left standing through winter: food and shelter in every month of the year.

    Habitat

  4. Build from close by.

    Sussex flint, local oak and reclaimed brick from within thirty miles. No plastic edging, no imported stone, nothing that will not age well.

    Local

How a garden gets made

Four stages and one team. The people who draw your plan are the people who lay the path, set out the plants and come back to cut the meadow in August.

01Design

Survey, scaled plan and a planting list made for your soil and light.

02Build

Paths, walls and ponds laid by our own crew from local materials.

03Plant

Peat-free, Sussex-grown plants, set out by the people who chose them.

04Tend

Seasonal visits that edit, prune and feed, so the garden keeps improving.

Room for wildlife

Every garden we make keeps a gap under the fence for hedgehogs, open water for frogs and newts, flowers from March to November for the bees, and seedheads left standing for the winter finches.

Last year our camera traps caught hedgehogs in 23 of the 41 gardens we look after.

What we build with

Nothing travels further than it has to. Most of what goes into a loam garden comes from within thirty miles of it.

  • Knapped flint

    For walls and edging, from Downland farms and the rubble of old barns.

    Within 8 miles

  • Sussex oak

    Green oak for raised beds, benches and pergolas, left to silver with age.

    Within 22 miles

  • Reclaimed brick

    Paths and low walls in bricks saved from local demolitions, cleaned by hand.

    Within 30 miles

  • Our own compost

    Three bays at the yard turn every clipping we take away into next year's mulch.

    At our yard

  • Hoggin and gravel

    Paths that let rain through, bound with clay instead of cement.

    Within 25 miles

Aftercare, all year round

Most new gardens fail in their third year, when nobody is looking. Ours are visited every season by the people who planted them.

Spring

March to May · three visits

  • Cut back last year's stems once the insects have left them
  • Mulch every bed with compost from our yard
  • Lift and divide crowded perennials

Summer

June to August · two visits

  • Cut meadow paths and the first hay in late July
  • Deadhead for a second flush, leave the rest to seed
  • Check ponds, butts and new trees through dry spells

Autumn

September to November · two visits

  • Plant bulbs, bare-root hedging and new trees
  • Rake leaves onto the beds instead of the bonfire
  • Sow yellow rattle into lawns turning to meadow

Winter

December to February · one visit

  • Prune fruit trees and lay the hedges
  • Leave seedheads and hollow stems standing
  • Walk the garden with you and plan the year

Questions we are asked

Anything else, ask us on the walk round. The first visit is free within twenty miles of Lewes.

How much does a new garden cost?

Most of our builds fall between £18,000 and £90,000. Design on its own starts at £1,400 for a small garden, and the fee comes off the build if we make it.

Where do you work?

Within about forty minutes of our yard in Lewes: Brighton and Hove, Seaford, Uckfield and the edge of the Weald. Aftercare keeps us close, so we do not travel further.

How long until it looks established?

Borders fill out in their second summer; hedges and trees take three to five years. We plan the first year so the garden never looks bare.

Can you take over a garden someone else made?

Yes. We look after it as it is for a full season first, then suggest changes once we know what thrives there.

Will you reuse what is already there?

Always, if it is healthy. Soil, stone, paving and plants on site are used before anything new is bought.
