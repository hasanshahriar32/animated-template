When the lights go down, the ceiling goes too.

The house lights fade over ninety seconds, the seats lean back thirty degrees, and then there is only sky: nine thousand stars, each one placed where it really stands above the city tonight.

Outward

Our flagship show runs fifty-two minutes and makes five stops, each one further from home than the last. The astronomer at the console decides how long we stay.

  1. 01

    Tonight’s sky

    Straight above the roof

    The sky over the city at ten tonight with every streetlight switched off: which planets are up and where to find them on the walk home.

  2. 02

    The Moon

    1.3 light-seconds away

    We drop low over the craters at the edge of the dark side, where shadows run for miles and the ground is older than any rock on Earth.

  3. 03

    The Sun

    8 light-minutes away

    Close enough to watch a flare lift off the surface, in the colours a space telescope sees and with a sound you will feel in the seat.

  4. 04

    The Milky Way

    100,000 light-years across

    We leave the Sun behind and turn to look back at our galaxy from the outside, a slow spiral with us somewhere in an outer arm.

  5. 05

    First galaxies

    13 billion years back

    The last stop is as far as light allows: galaxies caught colliding, and behind them the first ones that ever shone.

A ceiling that isn’t there

The dome is twenty-three metres across and tilted fifteen degrees toward the horizon, so the sky sits in front of you as well as overhead.

Two hundred and twelve seats lean back thirty degrees. Two laser projectors stitch the picture edge to edge, and the star projector in the middle of the room draws nine thousand stars as sharp points of light, never pixels.

“Nothing here is recorded. Ask the sky anything.”

Dr Ines Okafor, head of the dome

Every show is steered by hand by one of our six astronomers, who reads the room, slows down when the Moon gets a gasp and takes questions in the dark at the end.

At the console this month: Ines Okafor, Tomás Reyes, Hana Mori, Callum Reid, Priya Nair and Sofia Lindqvist. Each of them has a favourite stop, and you will hear which.

On clear Friday and Saturday nights the roof of the east tower slides back and a 40-centimetre telescope points wherever the astronomer on duty decides.

Before you lie back

Is it suitable for young children?

Small Sky is made for ages three to six and keeps the lights a little up. Every other show suits seven and over; babies in arms are welcome at Small Sky only.

What if I get motion sick?

Our flights are slow, but rows A to C at the back feel the least movement. Ask at the door for an aisle seat there, and look at the horizon line if you need a rest.

Can I come in after the start?

The doors close when the lights go down. If you are late, we move your ticket to the next show at no cost.

Is the dome accessible?

Step-free from the street to the seats, six wheelchair spaces, a hearing loop and a relaxed show with the lights half up on the first Sunday of every month.

What happens if the roof is clouded out?

The telescope evening moves indoors to a live tour of the same sky under the dome, and your roof pass stays valid for the next clear night.
