Every specimen is a moment someone kept still.

Since 1868 Wrenfield has gathered feathers, beetles, fossils, shells and seeds, each with a label saying who found it, where and when. Six halls show what the drawers hold; the rest waits in the stores for anyone who asks.

Walk the six halls

Six halls, one family tree.

Each hall follows one branch of life. Walk them in any order: the doors between them are always open.

Every plate on these walls was drawn from a specimen in our drawers, and the specimen is usually in the case beside it.

Flight

Hall 1 · Ground floor, east wing

Nine thousand birds, from a hummingbird the size of a thumbnail to the albatross that hangs over the stairs.

Insects

Hall 2 · Ground floor, west wing

Beetles, moths and the bees that keep the county fed: 2.1 million insects, the largest part of the collection.

Deep time

Hall 3 · Lower ground floor

Ammonites from the cliffs at Kingsbridge Bay, a mammoth tooth from the river gravels and four hundred million years in one walk.

Tide line

Hall 4 · First floor, east wing

Shells, corals and the animals of the rock pools, with a living tank of the shore at the far end of the hall.

Plants

Hall 5 · First floor, west wing

A herbarium of three hundred thousand pressed sheets, and the seed bank that keeps the county’s wild flowers alive.

Mammals

Hall 6 · Second floor

From the slow loris to the whale jaw above the landing: the animals we are most closely related to.

The Colour of Flight

Before photography, every bird in a book was engraved in black and coloured by hand, one wash at a time.

A hundred and twenty plates from 1790 to 1890, lent by eight libraries, show how colour learned to fly: the first blues that faded, the reds that never did, and the colourists whose names nobody wrote down.

Ninety-seven in every hundred specimens never go on show.

The halls hold three per cent of Wrenfield. The rest lives in 41,000 drawers on four floors of stores, catalogued and lent to researchers in thirty countries.

A third of the year, our curators are out there.

Wrenfield still collects: under licence, in small numbers, with the place and the hour written down. What our 38 scientists bring back goes into the drawers, and into the papers that show how the county is changing.

  • Chalk-stream mayflies

    River Wend, 2024 to 2027: counting each spring’s hatch against a survey made in 1931.

  • Shingle beetles

    Kingsbridge Bay, every April since 2011, on the same forty metres of beach.

  • The moor plots, again

    Hartmoor: re-surveying, square by square, the plant plots a vicar pegged out in 1912.

Before you come

Anything else, ask the front desk or write to us.

Is entry really free?

Yes. The six halls are free for everyone, every day we are open. Booking a free ticket only helps us plan busy days; you can always walk in.

Can I bring a pushchair or a wheelchair?

Every hall is step-free, with lifts to each floor. Pushchairs are welcome everywhere, and we lend wheelchairs and folding stools at the front desk.

Can I take photographs?

Yes, without flash, in the halls and The Colour of Flight. Tripods are fine on quiet mornings.

Will you identify something I found?

Bring it to the identification desk on the first Saturday of each month, 11:00 to 15:00. Bones, shells, fossils, insects and pressed plants all welcome.

Is there somewhere to eat?

The café on the landing serves lunch until 15:00, and the picnic room on the lower ground floor is open to school groups and families.

How do I give a specimen to the museum?

Write to the collections team with a photograph and where it was found. We accept most donations that come with a record of their place and date.
